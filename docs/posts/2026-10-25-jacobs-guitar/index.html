Fluent in the Language of Music

music
Author

Aleksandra

Published

October 25, 2026

For most people, including the person writing these words, language is the main form of expression. Luckily, language is not the only form of expression possible, and luckily we have artists who are fluent in these other forms1. Still, far fewer people learn to express themselves through music, dance or sculpture than acquire language. Artists often master their form of artistry until it becomes their primary form of expression. That said, calling art one’s primary form of expression is not quite the same as placing it on the same fundamental level as language. It was therefore striking to hear that a musician Jacob Collier say that he acquired music as a language.

I have heard musicians say that the sound of their instrument is their voice, or that from early childhood they were surrounded by music, or that they have played an instrument from such an early age that they don’t even remember being non-players. Fair enough, all strong statements. But only equating music with something as fundamental as language made me think what a profound experience it must be and how unparalleled it is as a mean of expression.

Let’s think for a moment about how we acquire the first language. Effortlessly. Fearlessly. Trying over and over again. Interacting with proficient speakers. Not being judged for making mistakes. Unconsciously acquiring fluency. Developing remarkable grammatical intuition, unmatched in any foreign language. In your native language grammar acts like a guardian angel, guiding you and protecting from mistakes. In a foreign language, by contrast, grammar resembles the mythical Cerberus who tries to bite you at every opportunity2. Also, notice this beautiful distinction between verbs: you don’t learn the mother tongue, you acquire it. And now imagine all these characteristics possible in the context of music. Fascinating, isn’t it?

In fact, even without equating music and language, the two have a lot in common: both are codified, they have grammar and they use a set of sounds. Someone wise once said that the difference between a language and a dialect is that a dialect is a language without an army. Music has no army, yet it is mighty. Music also has another significant advantage over all natural languages, in the sense that it is universal. Around the world, people with no musical training understand it at some fundamental level: we grasp the difference between high and low; between loud and quiet; between happy and sad. Moreover, we can also intuitively produce music: we can whistle a melody even though no one ever taught us how to move our lips, tongue and cheeks to produce different sounds.

These the thoughts have been occupying for more than a month. Exploring what music acquisition and language acquisition might have in common has been a delicious exercise. The trigger for all this was a Jacob Collier concert that I attended in September.

Before the concert, I knew Jacob Collier only through orchestral improvisations, which I found joyful, playful, ingenious, effortless, unbelievable and incomparable to any performances I heard before. So, when I learned that he was touring nearby, I wanted to expose my oldest son to an artist of exceptional musicality who enjoys what he is doing with every cell of his body. As for myself, to participate in these group improvisations, I had watched so many times on YouTube.

That was the goal. I bought the tickets half a year in advance and almost forgot about them. Then, the day before the concert, I read that it would be a one-man show. On top of that, it was said that in the album promoted by the tour Jacob has reinvented the guitar. The absence of an orchestra left me utterly disappointed, and the claim of reinvention only reinforced my negative attitude – I found it arrogant. However, I had the tickets, my son was excited about going to bed after midnight, and he just started playing the guitar himself - so what did we have to lose? Worst-case scenario, as Ryan Gosling and Emma Stone sang, “what a waste of lovely night”3.

Still, I hoped his exceptional musicality would shine, but my expectations, in the absence of an orchestra, had been lowered to the bare minimum.

I cannot remember the last time I was so happy to be proven wrong.

I am not going to describe the concert experience. There is a saying that a picture is worth a thousand words. In Jacob’s case, sound is worth a million. Nor am I going to dwell on how ignorant I was to know Jacob only through his orchestral performances. Shame on me. Instead, I am going to write about what I appreciated the most. In ascending order.

Firstly, I had been mourning the orchestra. Completely unnecessarily. Jacob is a one-man orchestra. He played multiple instruments, sang Bach, harmonized “Yesterday” as if it were Gregorian chant, he conjured rain from the audience. All by himself, all within two hours. He epitomizes diversity.

In fact, the tickets should carry a warning: “there is a high risk that this concert will leave a mark on you”. It was just contagious to watch and hear someone who loves what does so much and can express it so deeply. Even in the short time since the concert, I can see its positive effect on me: I practice my own instrument more.

Jacob’s concert sparked my son’s musical curiosity; my 9yo has referred to it on multiple occasions. And this is priceless.

Given this wealth of musical experience and tangible impact – and after a month of watching his earlier performances and interviews – I find it striking that what I value most is not Jacob Collier the musician. It is Jacob Collier the human being4. From those hours of interviews emerges the image of an extremely accomplished professional who blends a toddler’s curiosity with the self-awareness of someone with a PhD in psychology. His authenticity and willingness to try the unknown harmonize exceptionally well with a lack of ego and lack of agenda. Even individually, these qualities are remarkable; in an adult, any one of them is relatively rare and deserves praise. But the combination of so many of them in a single person is just mathematically impossible to realize on the planet Earth. And here he is, against the statistics. It is great to walk on this planet at the same time as you, Jacob Collier.

If you would like a glimpse of a curious, self-aware human being, watch this interview with Hank Green. Hank is a biochemist by training who became a comedian and much more. His interviews with artists follow an interesting format: it is the guest who asks questions to the host. So you can learn about the guests from the quality of the questions they ask. And, in Jacob’s own words5:

Questions are hugely underrated, as we are in the answers obsessed culture. (…) Good creative work poses more questions than it answers.

Could any message resonate better with the Excellent Problem blog?

And guess what – they did not print the warning in the concert description, but they were right about Jacob reinventing the guitar. He transposed the six-string instrument into a five-string one, covering the same tonal range but changing the intervals between the strings. The tuning makes the guitar tonally symmetrical. I do not play the guitar myself, but I can fully appreciate that different intervals between the strings make some combinations of notes easily accessible that are otherwise not playable on a classical guitar. Also, classical guitarist who have built all the muscle memory over the years of training, would have it difficult, least to say, to play anything on Jacob’s guitar.

At the end of the day a mere mortal has only five fingers, so how on earth is he supposed to play a six-string instrument? Thank you for reading.
